What the sea is

The surface is kinematic, not a Navier–Stokes solve. Each layer is a Gerstner train: particles orbit so crests sharpen and troughs flatten. Layers sum in world space. Wind follows the swell: chop grows on the windward face and crests, the lee is sheltered, and strong wind rips the tops off. Clicks drop localized impulses. The bench samples the line densely each frame so the hull can look up height in O(1).

  1. Represent. Wavelength, period, amplitude, and steepness become a parametric surface, not a height map painted by hand.
  2. Stack. Independent trains add. Gain, mute, and wind direction change the mix without rebuilding the field.
  3. Slice the hull. Twenty-five stations along the length each see a local waterline and a submerged section.
  4. Integrate. Heave and pitch follow from net force and moment — buoyancy minus damping — not from a scripted path.

The teaching model

Fᵢ = ρ g Δx · stripArea(beam, depthᵢ)

At station i, submerged depth is the overlap between the local hull section (keel to deck, pitched) and the interpolated wave. Strip area tapers for V-sections. Calibration scales total lift so that, at rest on flat water with the boot stripe on the surface, buoyancy equals weight.

Equilibrium solves a small sink offset by bisection and iterates pitch until the moment from the strip forces closes. Time stepping then uses heave (Fy − cy vy) / m and pitch (M − cp ωp) / I, with damping taken from hydrostatic stiffness. Seawater ρ = 1025 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s².

Honest limits

  • This is a real-time illustration, not CFD or a boundary-element code.
  • The sea does not feel the hull — no radiated or diffracted waves.
  • Section buoyancy sliders are classroom gains, not foil lift tables.

Gerstner sea

A Gerstner wave moves water particles in circles (or ellipses). The surface is not a vertical sine: crests peak and troughs stretch. Steepness ak is amplitude times wavenumber. Too steep, and the orbit overlaps — the lab then draws breaking tips.

A stack is several of these trains, plus optional wind chop and pointer impulses, summed at each sample.

Strip theory

The hull is cut into stations along its length. Each strip has its own submerged area and therefore its own buoyancy. The vertical forces sum to heave; the fore/aft imbalance is pitch moment. Early seakeeping work used the same idea: 2-D sections, not a full 3-D potential.

Heave and pitch

Heave is the vertical ride of the center of gravity relative to the still-waterline. Pitch is rotation about the beam. Trim is the steady bow-up / bow-down offset once the strips have settled. Immersion is how deep the keel sits; wetted is the fraction of stations still in the water.

Heading

From waves runs with the trains (following sea). Against waves runs into them (head sea). Encounter speed is wave celerity plus or minus ship speed; the scoreboard reports it as knots on λ when the sea is moving.

How to read a run

  1. Open Waves, pick a starter (Swell, Storm, …), Add to stack.
  2. Open Ship, pick a hull, set speed and heading.
  3. Watch Hs, period, and pitch. Click the sea for a local impulse.
  4. Raise bow section buoyancy and see the bow lift; raise stern to squat the bow.
  5. 3D is the same field on a mesh — not a second physics. Collapse the docks for a clean view.
